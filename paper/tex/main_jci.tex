\begin{abstract}
Adaptive assignment policies that increasingly favor one action, such as
softmax policies over reward-model scores, produce logs whose value for causal
inference depends on the effect being estimated. We price learning by
exploration loss: the cumulative value, measured in the deployer's own
objective, forgone whenever a non-preferred action is logged. In a model with
a one-dimensional score, Gaussian outcomes and Lipschitz mean functions, logs
from one shrinking temperature yield Wald intervals for the effect at the
policy's decision boundary that are asymptotically valid uniformly over the
model, while exploration loss tends to zero. In the same model, no design and
estimator can estimate the population average effect uniformly consistently
unless worst-case exploration loss diverges. The separation relies on the
operational cost vanishing at the boundary; with a fixed cost per deviation,
boundary inference also requires divergent loss. Without smoothness
restrictions, uniform precision for the population effect requires loss that,
as the target error shrinks, is of the order of the squared mean root gap
divided by the squared error, independent of sample size. A single common
temperature needs loss growing almost linearly in the sample size, whereas
uniform mixing keeps it bounded.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} adaptive experiments, limited overlap, regression
discontinuity, minimax lower bounds, generative AI, off-policy evaluation

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Many deployed systems choose actions with a policy that becomes more decisive
as it gains confidence: bandit algorithms, recommender and ranking systems,
algorithmic eligibility rules, and conversational AI systems that select a
response strategy through a softmax over reward-model scores. Their logs record
the action taken and often its probability. When such a policy is known and
increasingly deterministic, which causal effects can the logs deliver, and
what must be paid to learn the others?

Conversational AI makes the question concrete. Conversations with large language
models change political attitudes \cite{salvi2025conversational,hackenburg2025levers},
and the strategies such systems use, such as presenting evidence or asking a
question, are chosen adaptively. Regulation and replication concern the effects
of these strategies, and the logs of deployed systems are the evidence an
analyst inherits.

The answer depends on the target. We distinguish two:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item the \emph{boundary effect}, the effect among contexts where the policy is
indifferent between the two actions; and
\item the \emph{population average effect} over all contexts the deployment
serves.
\end{itemize}
The first is the effect for units at the margin of the current policy, the
quantity that governs small changes to the policy's threshold, in the spirit of
marginal policy-relevant treatment effects \cite{carneiro2010evaluating}; the
second is the average outcome change from assigning one action rather than the
other across the whole context population. Estimating one well does not
substitute for estimating the other.

We price learning in the deployer's own currency. Each time the logged action
differs from the one the deployer's objective prefers, the deployer gives up an
operational value gap $g(h)$; the cumulative sum is the \emph{exploration loss}
$R_n$. For a system that chooses by a score, the natural cost of an exploratory
action is the score difference it forgoes: small where the policy is nearly
indifferent, large where it is confident. This cost is defined by the deployer's
objective rather than by the outcome under study, so it can be computed at the
design stage.

This paper gives lower bounds stated in exploration loss that hold for every
estimator and every, possibly adaptive, design, and pairs them with boundary
inference in the same model. The combination shows that, under one sharpening
temperature, the boundary effect admits valid inference while cumulative
exploration loss tends to zero, whereas the population effect cannot be learned
uniformly without divergent loss. Its ingredients are familiar, namely limited
overlap, local randomization near a threshold and cost-weighted allocation; the
contribution is to place both targets in one model on one cost scale, and to
make precise when the separation fails. It depends on the cost vanishing at the
boundary: if every deviation carries a fixed cost, boundary inference also
requires divergent loss (Proposition~\ref{prop:gapshape}).

Figure~\ref{fig:separation} shows the construction behind the population-side
lower bound. Two outcome models agree near the boundary and differ only for the
unpreferred action away from it; a sharpening policy almost never takes that
action there.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \emph{Separation in one model} (Theorems~\ref{thm:boundary}
and~\ref{thm:separation}). With Gaussian outcomes and globally Lipschitz mean
functions, a common temperature $\tau_n=n^{-\zeta}$, $\zeta\in(1/2,1)$, gives
Wald intervals for the boundary effect that are asymptotically valid uniformly
over the model while $R_n\to0$; in the same model, uniformly consistent
estimation of the population effect requires $\sup R_n\to\infty$, and there are
two members with equal boundary effects that no estimator distinguishes when
$R_n\to0$.
\item \emph{An information--exploration inequality} (Theorem~\ref{thm:info})
for all estimators and adaptive designs in the model of bounded mean functions,
attained in order by a gap-based design (Proposition~\ref{prop:achieve}).
\item \emph{Consequences for design} (Section~\ref{sec:temperature}): one common
temperature is costly for the population effect, while uniform mixing,
context-dependent temperatures and suitable temperature mixtures are not; and
the shape of the operational gap determines whether boundary inference is cheap
(Proposition~\ref{prop:gapshape}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.} All results concern one decision per unit, with independent
contexts and a known assignment probability. The score $\Delta$ and the
preferred action are fixed functions of the context: they are not learned from
the outcomes, so ``adaptive'' refers to sharpening and to designs whose
probabilities may depend on past data, not to a bandit that learns which action
is better. The boundary results use a one-dimensional score. The lower bounds
assume Gaussian outcomes with known variance and hold in models without
extrapolation structure: where data near the boundary identify the population
effect, as under a constant effect, the separation disappears. Multi-turn
conversations, estimated or learned scores and effects of realized text are
outside the formal results.

\paragraph{Organization.} Section~\ref{sec:setup} sets out the model and
Section~\ref{sec:brief} states the results informally.
Sections~\ref{sec:boundary}--\ref{sec:separation} give boundary inference, the
population lower bounds and the separation in one model;
Section~\ref{sec:temperature} treats temperature as a design lever;
Section~\ref{sec:sims} reports simulations; Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
concludes. Proofs are in the appendix.

\paragraph{Relation to existing work.}
\emph{Boundary estimands and local randomization.} Lee~\cite{lee2008randomized}
interprets regression discontinuity as local randomization under imprecise
control of the running variable. Abdulkadiro\u{g}lu et
al.~\cite{abdulkadiroglu2017research,abdulkadiroglu2022breaking} use propensity
scores induced by assignment mechanisms, including local propensity scores near
tie-breaking cutoffs, and Narita and Yata~\cite{narita2021algorithm} show that
overlap-type weights built from an approximate propensity score of a known
algorithm converge to a boundary estimand and require undersmoothing; these are
the closest antecedents for Theorem~\ref{thm:boundary}. There the smoothing is
chosen by the analyst in covariate space; here it is a temperature chosen by the
system in score space, and the exploration loss is computed jointly with the
inferential guarantee. Eckles et al.~\cite{eckles2020noise} use known assignment
noise near a cutoff with a fixed noise scale. Overlap weights and their target
population are due to Li, Morgan and Zaslavsky~\cite{li2018balancing}, and
Humphreys~\cite{humphreys2025bounds} shows that the fixed-effects estimand weights
stratum effects by the assignment variance $p_j(1-p_j)$, the same weighting,
which under a sharpening policy concentrates on the decision boundary.

\emph{Exploration and estimation.} Trade-offs between reward and estimation error
appear for multi-armed bandits in Erraqabi et al.~\cite{erraqabi2017trading} and
Simchi-Levi and Wang~\cite{simchilevi2023multi}. In a non-contextual
setting, Liang and Bojinov~\cite{liang2023mixture} mix any bandit algorithm with
a Bernoulli design whose weight vanishes slowly, keeping anytime-valid inference
on the average effect; our recommendation of uniform mixing has the same logic. Inference from adaptively collected
data with vanishing propensities is treated by Hadad et
al.~\cite{hadad2021confidence}; adaptive designs for treatment effects and for
policy choice by Kato et al.~\cite{kato2020efficient} and Kasy and
Sautmann~\cite{kasy2021adaptive}, with applications in political science
reviewed by Offer-Westort, Coppock and Green~\cite{offerwestort2021adaptive}.
Conservative bandits keep cumulative reward above a baseline
policy's~\cite{wu2016conservative}, and Wan, Kveton and Song~\cite{wan2022safe}
optimize exploration for policy evaluation under a value constraint. Tucker and
Joachims~\cite{tucker2023variance} design logging policies that augment existing
logs to reduce off-policy evaluation variance, and Douglas, Persson and
Provost~\cite{douglas2026logging} derive optimal logging policies for
inverse-propensity estimation and report that softmax logging performs worse than
top-$k$ logging in simulation. Koppel et al.~\cite{koppel2025explore} extend
inverse-gap-weighting contextual bandits to balance regret against the quality of
average-treatment-effect estimates. The
allocation $q\propto g^{-1/2}$ has the form of optimum stratified allocation with
costs \cite{cochran1977sampling}, and Ravichandran et
al.~\cite{ravichandran2024optimal} derive the analogous cost-weighted rule for
factorial experiments. We do not claim the allocation as new; what we add is that
the corresponding constant bounds every estimator and adaptive design when loss
is paid only for non-preferred actions. Li and Imai~\cite{li2024neyman} compare
evaluating an individualized treatment rule by randomizing units to the rule with
evaluation from a standard experiment, without a cost for assignments against the
rule. Cesa-Bianchi et al.~\cite{cesabianchi2017boltzmann} show that Boltzmann
exploration with a common schedule can fail for regret; Theorem~\ref{thm:common}
is an estimation counterpart.

\emph{Limited overlap and off-policy evaluation.} Lower bounds for off-policy
value estimation \cite{li2015toward,wang2017optimal,mou2023off} and limited-overlap
asymptotics and trimming
\cite{khan2010irregular,crump2009dealing,rothe2017robust,hong2020inference,ma2020robust}
supply the information-theoretic ingredients; the collapse of inverse weighting
under shrinking propensities is irregular identification in the sense of Khan and
Tamer~\cite{khan2010irregular}. Ring and Schomaker~\cite{ring2026diagnostic}
diagnose stochastic positivity problems and use the diagnosis to choose
alternative estimands; our results give a formal counterpart for known,
sharpening policies, stating which estimand remains learnable and at what cost.
Khan, Saveski and Ugander~\cite{khan2024off} bound effects without overlap under
smoothness, the kind of extrapolation structure our impossibility excludes.

\emph{Generative treatments.} Athey, Imbens and Ji~\cite{athey2026stochastic}
identify effects of content features from stochastic algorithms using logged
unexposed candidates and probability ratios; their estimand averages over
co-occurring pairs with trimming and does not conflict with our bounds. Fresh and
Shin~\cite{fresh2026conversation} give a taxonomy of conversational estimands, and
Imai and Nakamura~\cite{imai2026causal,nakamura2026dynamic} study effects of
realized text features; we work at the level of strategy assignment with the
generator held fixed.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Treatments.} Units $i=1,\dots,n$ arrive with contexts $H_i\sim F$.
The system chooses a strategy $A_i\in\{0,1\}$ and produces a message
$M_i\sim G(\cdot\mid A_i,H_i)$ from a fixed generation kernel $G$, which includes
token-level sampling settings; the outcome is $Y_i$. The treatment is the
assignment of the strategy with $G$ held fixed, so potential outcomes $Y_i(a)$
include the randomness of the message
\cite{munoz2012population,young2014identification}; changing $G$ changes the
treatment. We assume:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(C\arabic*),leftmargin=3em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{consistency}: $Y_i=Y_i(A_i)$;
\item \emph{independent units}: $(H_i,Y_i(0),Y_i(1))$ are independent and
identically distributed across $i$, with no interference;
\item \emph{sequential ignorability with known probabilities}: $A_i$ is independent
of $(Y_i(0),Y_i(1))$ given $(H_i,\mathcal F_{i-1})$, where $\mathcal F_{i-1}$ is
the data of units $1,\dots,i-1$, and $\Prob(A_i=1\mid H_i,\mathcal F_{i-1})$ is known.
\end{enumerate}
Then the law of $Y_i$ given $(A_i=a,H_i=h,\mathcal F_{i-1})$ is that of $Y(a)$
given $H=h$, so statements about conditional outcome laws are statements about
potential outcomes.

\paragraph{Deployer.} The deployer has a score gap $\Delta(h)=s_1(h)-s_0(h)$
from its own objective, a preferred (greedy) action $a^*(h)=\mathbf 1\{\Delta(h)>0\}$,
and an operational value gap $g(h)\ge0$, the value its objective loses by choosing
$\bar a(h)=1-a^*(h)$. We write $g=\phi(|\Delta|)$ when the gap is a function of the
score. The score and the preferred action are fixed functions of the context.

\paragraph{Running example.} A conversational system chooses between presenting
evidence ($A=1$) and asking a question ($A=0$). A reward model scores both
strategies given the conversation so far, $H$; $\Delta(H)$ is the score difference,
and the system logs the probability with which it chose evidence. The operational
gap $g(H)$ is the loss in the deployer's objective, for example predicted
engagement, from using the lower-scoring strategy. The outcome $Y$ is the person's
attitude after the conversation, measured separately and not used by the policy.

\paragraph{Probabilities and designs.} We write
\[
e(h)=\Prob(A=1\mid H=h),\qquad q(h)=\Prob(A\neq a^*(h)\mid H=h)
\]
for the probability of action $1$ and of the non-preferred action. A design is
non-adaptive if these probabilities do not depend on past data. A \emph{common
temperature} design uses one deterministic $\tau=\tau_n$ and
\[
e_\tau(h)=\sgm(\Delta(h)/\tau),\qquad q_\tau(h)=\sgm(-|\Delta(h)|/\tau),
\qquad \sgm(x)=1/(1+\exp(-x)).
\]

\paragraph{Exploration loss.} The expected cumulative operational loss is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss-definition}
 R_n=\E\left[\sum_{i=1}^n g(H_i)\mathbf 1\{A_i=\bar a(H_i)\}\right],
\end{equation}
and $R_n=n\E[g(H)q(H)]$ for non-adaptive designs. Under adaptive designs $R_n$
may depend on the outcome law through past observations.

\paragraph{Estimands.} Let $\mu_a(h)=\E[Y(a)\mid H=h]$ and $c=\mu_1-\mu_0$. The
population average effect is $\theta=\E[c(H)]$. With a one-dimensional score
$\Delta(h)=h$ the boundary effect is $\beta_0=c(0)$. For a given temperature, the
overlap-weighted effect
$\beta_{\ov,\tau}=\E[\omega_\tau(H)c(H)]/\E[\omega_\tau(H)]$, with
$\omega_\tau=e_\tau(1-e_\tau)$ \cite{li2018balancing}, is an intermediate target.

\section{The result in brief}\label{sec:brief}

Take $\Delta(h)=h$, Gaussian outcomes with variance $\sigma^2$, and mean functions
bounded by $B$ and $L$-Lipschitz; call this model $\Mlip$. Let the logs come from
one common temperature $\tau_n=n^{-\zeta}$ with $\zeta\in(1/2,1)$, and let the
operational gap vanish linearly at the boundary.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item The Wald interval for the boundary effect is asymptotically valid uniformly
over $\Mlip$, and the exploration loss $R_n$ tends to zero
(Theorems~\ref{thm:boundary} and~\ref{thm:separation}).
\item In $\Mlip$, there exist two outcome laws with equal boundary effects and
different population effects that differ only for the non-preferred action in a
region where the operational gap is bounded below; no estimator is consistent for
the population effect at both whenever $R_n\to0$. Uniformly consistent estimation
of the population effect over $\Mlip$ requires divergent worst-case loss, for
every design and estimator (Theorem~\ref{thm:separation}).
\item In the larger model of all bounded mean functions, uniform precision
$\delta$ for the population effect requires worst-case loss of order at least
$(\E\sqrt g)^2/\delta^2$ as $\delta\to0$, and this order does not depend on $n$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:info}).
\end{enumerate}
If instead every deviation carries a fixed cost, (i) fails as well
(Proposition~\ref{prop:gapshape}). Figure~\ref{fig:map} shows the separation for
the simulation design of Section~\ref{sec:sims}. Under one common temperature the
population effect reaches root mean squared error $\figDelta$ only where the
exploration loss is large, at least about $\figPopMinLoss$ on the plotted grid,
whereas the boundary effect reaches it with loss $\figBndMinLoss$ at the edge of
the grid and with less at larger $n$.

\section{Boundary effects at vanishing exploration loss}\label{sec:boundary}

\begin{assumption}[Boundary model]\label{ass:D}
$\Delta(h)=h$, $a^*(h)=\mathbf 1\{h>0\}$, and
\begin{enumerate}[label=(D\arabic*),leftmargin=3em,itemsep=1pt]
\item $H$ has a density $f\le\bar f$, continuous at $0$, with $f(0)>0$;
\item $|\mu_a|\le B$, and $\mu_a$ is $L$-Lipschitz on $[-u_0,u_0]$;
\item $\sigma_a^2(h)=\Var(Y\mid A=a,H=h)$ is continuous at $0$ with
$\sigma_1^2(0)+\sigma_0^2(0)>0$, and $\E[Y^4\mid A,H]\le M_4$;
\item $\Prob(A=1\mid H=h)=\sgm(h/\tau_n)$ with $\tau_n\to0$, known;
\item $g=\phi(|h|)\le\bar G$ with $\phi(u)\le\bar\kappa u$ on $[0,u_0]$; in the
strong form $\phi'(0)=\kappa>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

With $e_i=e_{\tau_n}(H_i)$, define
\begin{align}
 w_{1i}&=A_i(1-e_i), & w_{0i}&=(1-A_i)e_i,\nonumber\\
 \hat D_a&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n w_{ai}, &
 \hat m_a&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{ai}Y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_{ai}},
 &\hat\beta&=\hat m_1-\hat m_0,\label{eq:boundary-estimator}\\
 \hat\psi_i&=\frac{w_{1i}(Y_i-\hat m_1)}{\hat D_1}
             -\frac{w_{0i}(Y_i-\hat m_0)}{\hat D_0}, &
 \hat s^2&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\hat\psi_i^2. \label{eq:boundary-se}
\end{align}
If either weight sum is zero, set $\hat\beta=\hat s^2=0$ and report the interval
$\mathbb R$; this event has probability tending to zero when $n\tau_n\to\infty$.
Write $m_\tau=\E[\omega_\tau(H)]$, $\bar\mu_a=\E[\omega_\tau(H)\mu_a(H)]/m_\tau$,
\[
\xi=\frac{w_1(Y-\bar\mu_1)-w_0(Y-\bar\mu_0)}{m_\tau},\qquad s_n^2=\Var(\xi),
\]
so that $\bar\mu_1-\bar\mu_0=\beta_{\ov,\tau}$ and $\xi$ is the linearization of
$\hat\beta-\beta_{\ov,\tau}$.

\begin{theorem}[Boundary inference]\label{thm:boundary}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:D}, if $n\tau_n\to\infty$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item $(\hat\beta-\beta_{\ov,\tau_n})/(s_n/\sqrt n)\Rightarrow N(0,1)$, and
$s_n^2=\{\sigma_1^2(0)+\sigma_0^2(0)\}/\{2\tau_nf(0)\}\,(1+o(1))$;
\item $|\beta_{\ov,\tau_n}-\beta_0|\le C\tau_n$ for large $n$, for every
$C>4\log2\,L\bar f/f(0)$;
\item $\hat s^2/s_n^2\to1$ in probability;
\item if also $n\tau_n^3\to0$, the Wald interval $\hat\beta\pm
z_{1-\eta/2}\hat s/\sqrt n$ has asymptotic coverage $1-\eta$ for $\beta_0$;
\item $R_n=n\E[g(H)q_{\tau_n}(H)]\le n\tau_n^2\bar\kappa\bar f\pi^2/6+n\bar G\exp(-u_0/\tau_n)$, and in
the strong form $R_n=n\tau_n^2\kappa f(0)\pi^2/6\,(1+o(1))$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, for $\tau_n=n^{-\zeta}$ with $\zeta\in(1/2,1)$, the interval in
(iv) is asymptotically valid and $R_n\to0$.
\end{theorem}

The three rate conditions are $n\tau_n\to\infty$ (the boundary sample grows,
$\zeta<1$), $n\tau_n^3\to0$ (bias is negligible relative to the standard error,
$\zeta>1/3$) and $n\tau_n^2\to0$ (the loss vanishes, $\zeta>1/2$). The error is
$|\hat\beta-\beta_0|=O_p((n\tau_n)^{-1/2}+\tau_n)$; we do not claim this rate is
optimal. With $\zeta=1/2$ the interval remains valid and the loss stays bounded.
Differentiability of $c$ at zero does not improve the bias order in general: a
bounded effect equal to $1+|h|^{3/2}$ near zero gives bias of order $\tau^{3/2}$.
If $c$ is $C^{1,1}$ and $f$ is Lipschitz near zero, the bias is $O(\tau^2)$ and
condition (iv) relaxes to $n\tau_n^5\to0$. Proofs are in
Appendix~\ref{app:boundary}.

Vanishing loss does not mean that exploration disappears. The expected number of
non-preferred actions is $n\E[q_{\tau_n}(H)]=2\log2\,f(0)\,n\tau_n(1+o(1))$, which
grows; the loss vanishes because these actions occur ever closer to the boundary,
where the operational gap is small. The next result makes the role of the gap's
shape explicit.

\begin{proposition}[Shape of the operational gap]\label{prop:gapshape}
Let (D1) and (D4) hold and $g=\phi(|h|)\le\bar G$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item If $\phi(u)\le\bar\kappa u^p$ on $[0,u_0]$ for some $p>0$, then
$R_n\le 2\bar\kappa\bar f C_p\,n\tau_n^{p+1}+n\bar G\exp(-u_0/\tau_n)$ with
$C_p=\int_0^\infty v^p\sgm(-v)\,dv<\infty$. Under the other conditions of
Theorem~\ref{thm:boundary}, $\tau_n=n^{-\zeta}$ with
$\max\{1/3,1/(p+1)\}<\zeta<1$ gives a valid interval for $\beta_0$ and $R_n\to0$.
\item If $\phi(u)\ge\lambda_0>0$ for $u\in(0,u_0]$, then
$R_n\ge2\log2\,\lambda_0f(0)\,n\tau_n(1+o(1))$, which diverges whenever
$n\tau_n\to\infty$, the condition under which Theorem~\ref{thm:boundary} applies.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Part (b) covers any cost incurred per deviation irrespective of context, such as
a review or a payment. Under such costs the boundary effect is no cheaper to
learn than the population effect, and the separation below does not hold in this
form. The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:boundary}.

\section{Population effects require paid exploration}\label{sec:population}

Fix $B>0$ and $\sigma>0$. Let $\M(B,\sigma)$ be the model in which
$Y\mid A=a,H=h$ is $N(\mu_a(h),\sigma^2)$ and $\mu_0,\mu_1$ are \emph{any}
measurable functions bounded by $B$. The model contains no structure that carries
effects learned near the boundary to other contexts.

\begin{theorem}[Information--exploration inequality]\label{thm:info}
Let $S_1,\dots,S_J$ be disjoint sets with $f_j=F(S_j)>0$, each contained in
$\{a^*=0\}$ or in $\{a^*=1\}$, and $g_j=\essinf_{S_j}g$. For every design,
adaptive or not, and every estimator $\hat\theta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:info}
\sup_{\M(B,\sigma)}\E(\hat\theta-\theta)^2\cdot
\Big(\sup_{\M(B,\sigma)}R_n+\frac{\sigma^2\pi^2}{B^2}\sum_j g_j\Big)
\;\ge\;\sigma^2\Big(\sum_jf_j\sqrt{g_j}\Big)^2 .
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $F(g\ge\gamma)>0$ for some $\gamma>0$, uniform consistency
requires $\sup R_n\to\infty$; and if $\sup\E(\hat\theta-\theta)^2\le\delta_n^2\to0$,
then $\liminf_n\delta_n^2\sup R_n\ge\sigma^2(\E\sqrt{g(H)})^2$.
\end{theorem}

Neither side of \eqref{eq:info} involves $n$: deployment scale does not substitute
for exploration when the target is the population effect. At a fixed precision
$\delta$, \eqref{eq:info} gives
$\sup R_n\ge\sigma^2(\sum_jf_j\sqrt{g_j})^2/\delta^2-\sigma^2\pi^2\sum_jg_j/B^2$,
whose best value over partitions is at most $\sigma^2(\E\sqrt g)^2/\delta^2$;
the constant $(\E\sqrt g)^2$ is reached only as $\delta\to0$. If $\sum_jg_j>0$, at
$R_n=0$ the bound is the finite floor $B^2(\sum_jf_j\sqrt{g_j})^2/(\pi^2\sum_jg_j)$,
consistent with the bounded error of a constant estimator.

\begin{proposition}[Achievability]\label{prop:achieve}
Suppose $0<\E\sqrt g<\infty$, $\delta_n>0$ and $n\delta_n^2\to\infty$. Write
$C=\sigma^2+B^2$. For $n\delta_n^2>4C$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:allocation}
 c_n=\frac{\E\sqrt g}{n\delta_n^2/C-4},\qquad
 q_n(h)=\begin{cases}
 \min\{1/2,c_n/\sqrt{g(h)}\},&g(h)>0,\\
 1/2,&g(h)=0,
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and let $e_n(h)$ be the corresponding probability of action $1$. The
Horvitz--Thompson estimator
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ht}
 \hat\theta_{\rm HT}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
 \left\{\frac{A_iY_i}{e_n(H_i)}-
 \frac{(1-A_i)Y_i}{1-e_n(H_i)}\right\}
\end{equation}
has $\sup_{\M(B,\sigma)}\E(\hat\theta_{\rm HT}-\theta)^2\le\delta_n^2$ and
$R_n\le Cn(\E\sqrt g)^2/(n\delta_n^2-4C)=C(\E\sqrt g)^2\delta_n^{-2}(1+o(1))$.
If also $\E[g]<\infty$, uniform mixing with $q\equiv C/(n\delta_n^2-2C)$, for
$n\delta_n^2\ge4C$, has the same uniform risk guarantee and
$R_n=Cn\E[g]/(n\delta_n^2-2C)=C\E[g]\delta_n^{-2}(1+o(1))$.
\end{proposition}

The order $\delta^{-2}$ and the functional $(\E\sqrt g)^2$ appear in both the
lower bound and the attainable loss, but the constants differ: the lower bound has
$\sigma^2$ and the Horvitz--Thompson upper bound $\sigma^2+B^2$, a factor that can
be large when $B^2\gg\sigma^2$. We do not establish the optimal constant. Proofs are
in Appendix~\ref{app:population}.

\section{The separation in one model}\label{sec:separation}

Theorem~\ref{thm:info} is proved in $\M(B,\sigma)$, which is unrestricted away
from the boundary. A lower bound over a large model does not transfer to a smaller
one, so we state both sides in a model with global smoothness. Fix $F$ satisfying
(D1), $g=\phi(|h|)$ satisfying (D5), and $B,L,\sigma>0$. Let $\Mlip$ be the set of
outcome laws with $Y\mid A=a,H=h\sim N(\mu_a(h),\sigma^2)$ and $\mu_0,\mu_1$ bounded
by $B$ and $L$-Lipschitz on $\mathbb R$. Every member of $\Mlip$ satisfies (D2)
and (D3).

\begin{assumption}\label{ass:SA}
There are $0<u_1<u_2$ with $F([u_1,u_2])>0$ and
$\gamma:=\essinf_{h\in[u_1,u_2]}g(h)>0$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{theorem}[Separation]\label{thm:separation}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:SA}, let
$b(h)=\sin^2(\pi(h-u_1)/(u_2-u_1))\mathbf 1\{u_1\le h\le u_2\}$,
$t_{\max}=\min\{B,L(u_2-u_1)/\pi\}$, and for $|t|\le t_{\max}$ let $\mu^t$ have
$\mu^t_1\equiv0$ and $\mu^t_0=-tb$. Then $\mu^t\in\Mlip$, all $\mu^t$ coincide on
$(-\infty,u_1]$, $\beta_0(\mu^t)=0$ and $\theta(\mu^t)=t\E[b(H)]$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(S\arabic*),leftmargin=3em,itemsep=2pt]
\item Under the common-temperature design $\tau_n=n^{-\zeta}$, $\zeta\in(1/2,1)$,
$R_n\to0$ and
$\sup_{\mu\in\Mlip}\big|P_\mu\{\beta_0(\mu)\in\hat\beta\pm z_{1-\eta/2}\hat s/\sqrt n\}-(1-\eta)\big|\to0$.
\item For every design and every $t$,
$\TV(P^n_{\mu^0},P^n_{\mu^t})\le R_n(\mu^0)/\gamma$. Hence if $R_n(\mu^0)\to0$,
for every $t_1\in(0,t_{\max}]$ and every estimator,
$\max_{t\in\{0,t_1\}}P_{\mu^t}(|\hat\theta_n-\theta(\mu^t)|\ge t_1\E[b]/2)\ge
(1-R_n(\mu^0)/\gamma)/2\to1/2$.
\item For every design and every estimator,
\[
\sup_{\Mlip}\E(\hat\theta-\theta)^2\ge\frac{(\E[b])^2}{\sup_{\Mlip}R_n/(\sigma^2\gamma)+\pi^2/t_{\max}^2}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Both sides are now uniform over the same model: (S1) gives boundary intervals whose
coverage converges uniformly, and (S3) shows that uniform consistency for the
population effect requires unbounded worst-case loss. (S2) adds a statement at two
fixed members. Because $\theta\in[-2B,2B]$, uniform consistency in probability and
in mean square are equivalent here after clipping an estimator to that interval.
We do not claim that the constant of Theorem~\ref{thm:info} holds in $\Mlip$. If a
submodel lets boundary data identify $\theta$, the perturbation family is excluded
and (S2)--(S3) do not apply. Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:separation}.

\section{Temperature as an exploration device}\label{sec:temperature}

\begin{theorem}[One common temperature]\label{thm:common}
Consider common-temperature designs with one deterministic $\tau_n$ per $n$.
Assume $g=\phi(|\Delta|)$ with $\E[g]<\infty$; $|\Delta(H)|$ has a density at least
$f_{\min}>0$ on $[0,v_0]$; $\phi(u)\ge\underline\kappa u$ on $[0,v_0]$; and
$D_{\max}=\esssup|\Delta(H)|<\infty$. Fix $\epsilon\in(0,D_{\max})$ and let
$\nu_\epsilon=\max_j\Prob(|\Delta(H)|\ge D_{\max}-\epsilon,\ a^*(H)=j)$, which is
positive. If an estimator has $\sup_{\M(B,\sigma)}\E(\hat\theta-\theta)^2\le\delta_n^2$
with $\delta_n^2\le\nu_\epsilon^2B^2/(2\pi^2)$ and $2n\delta_n^2>\nu_\epsilon\sigma^2$,
then
\[
R_n\ \ge\ \underline\kappa f_{\min}c_*\,n\min\Big\{v_0^2,\ \frac{(D_{\max}-\epsilon)^2}{\log^2\{2n\delta_n^2/(\nu_\epsilon\sigma^2)\}}\Big\},
\qquad c_*=\int_0^1v\,\sgm(-v)\,dv .
\]
\end{theorem}

This is a lower bound, and $\delta_n$ may be fixed. For fixed $\delta$ with
$\delta^2\le\nu_\epsilon^2B^2/(2\pi^2)$, the common-temperature loss is at least of
order $n/\log^2n$, whereas Proposition~\ref{prop:achieve} attains the same uniform
precision with loss bounded in $n$. The precision condition cannot be dropped: for
$\delta\ge2B$ the estimator that always outputs zero meets the target with no
exploration. The theorem is stated in the unrestricted model $\M(B,\sigma)$; with
extrapolation structure, such as a correctly specified parametric outcome model, a
model-based estimator can do better. The reason for the cost is not that a common
temperature explores in the wrong place, since the optimal design also explores most
near the boundary; it is that the probability of the non-preferred action decays
exponentially in $|\Delta|$, so observations far from the boundary become too rare
unless the temperature is raised everywhere.

\begin{remark}[Mixtures of temperatures]\label{rem:mixture}
Theorem~\ref{thm:common} does not extend to random mixtures of finite temperatures.
With $|\Delta|=|H|$, $H\sim U(-1,1)$ and $g=|H|$, let $r(u)=\sgm(-u)$ be the
non-preferred probability at temperature $1$, and mix temperature $1$ with weight
$w_n=\E[1/r(|H|)]/\{n\delta^2/(\sigma^2+B^2)-2\}$ and temperature $1/n$ otherwise.
The Horvitz--Thompson estimator then has uniform mean squared error at most
$\delta^2$ over $\M(B,\sigma)$, and the loss is at most
$(\sigma^2+B^2)\E[1/r]\E[|H|r]/\delta^2+O(1/n)$, bounded in $n$; at $\delta=0.1$ the
factor $\E[1/r]\E[|H|r]/\delta^2$ equals \mixtureCost{}. The lesson concerns a single
common temperature, not temperature as such.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Context temperatures]\label{prop:ctx}
For $\Delta(h)\neq0$ and $q(h)\in(0,1/2)$, the context temperature
$\tau(h)=|\Delta(h)|/\log\{(1-q(h))/q(h)\}$ gives non-preferred probability $q(h)$;
$q(h)=1/2$ requires $\tau(h)=\infty$, and on $\{\Delta=0\}$ every temperature gives
$q=1/2$. If $g=\phi(|\Delta|)$ with $\phi(0)=0$, the design $q_n$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:achieve} is implementable with temperatures in $(0,\infty]$,
and for each $h$ with $\Delta(h)\neq0$ and $g(h)>0$, $\tau_n(h)\log(1/c_n)\to|\Delta(h)|$.
\end{proposition}

The limit is pointwise. Where the cap binds, for example $0<|h|\le4c_n^2$ when
$g(h)=|h|$, the implementing temperature is $\tau=\infty$. When the gap is known
only up to a multiplicative error, a Kantorovich bound (Lemma~\ref{lem:robust} in
Appendix~\ref{app:temperature}) gives the worst-case cost of allocating by the
misspecified gap relative to the correct one, in a limit of large inverse
exploration moment. Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:temperature}.

\section{Simulations}\label{sec:sims}

The simulations use $H\sim U(-1,1)$, $\Delta(h)=h$, $g(h)=|h|$, $\mu_0(h)=h$,
$c(h)=1+|h|$ (population effect $1.5$, boundary effect $1$) and $N(0,1)$ noise.
Code, seeds and result files are in the repository cited in the data availability
statement.

\paragraph{Exploration designs.} For allocation comparisons we match the
sparse-exploration criterion $V_{\rm sp}(q)=\sigma^2\E[1/q]/n$, an allocation
criterion rather than the mean squared error of any estimator. At $n=10^5$ and
$V_{\rm sp}=0.01$, the closed-form exploration loss is \costUniform{} for uniform
mixing, \costOptimal{} for the gap-based design $q\propto g^{-1/2}$ (calibrated to
$V_{\rm sp}$ and capped at $1/2$, rather than the $c_n$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:achieve}), and \costCommon{} for a common temperature
($\tau=\costCommonTau$), a ratio of \costRatioCommonUniform{} to uniform mixing
(Figure~\ref{fig:costs}; Figure~\ref{fig:costs-small} in Appendix~\ref{app:figures}
repeats it at $V_{\rm sp}=0.03^2$). Table~\ref{tab:designs} runs the augmented
inverse-propensity estimator with a working model linear in $(1,H,|H|)$ separately
in each arm, which is correctly specified for this design, on common random numbers
across designs; the context-temperature implementation gives identical rows. At
fixed $V_{\rm sp}$ the expected number of non-preferred actions stays bounded for
uniform mixing and the gap-based design, so their nominal Wald coverage has no
asymptotic justification as $n$ grows; this does not apply to the common
temperature. In a two-context example with masses $(0.5,0.5)$, gaps $(0.2,1)$,
$\sigma=1$, $B=5$ and $\delta=0.1$, the lower bound \eqref{eq:info} evaluates to
\finiteBound{} against the asymptotic constant \finiteConstant{}, and the
common-temperature loss has local growth exponent \finiteSlope{} against the
predicted $1-\Delta_{\min}/\Delta_{\max}=0.8$.

\paragraph{A regression-discontinuity benchmark.} The estimator of
Theorem~\ref{thm:boundary} uses the known assignment probabilities and only the
$\tau$-neighbourhood of the boundary. A fuzzy local-linear regression-discontinuity
estimator at $\Delta=0$ ignores the probabilities and uses a bandwidth chosen by the
analyst. Table~\ref{tab:rd} in Appendix~\ref{app:figures} compares the two at
$n=10^5$ on the same logs. At $1/\tau\le30$ neither is reliable: the estimator of
Theorem~\ref{thm:boundary} is centred at $\beta_{\ov,\tau}$ rather than $\beta_0$,
and the discontinuity estimator has a weak first stage within the bandwidth. At
$1/\tau=120$ both cover at close to the nominal level; with the wider bandwidth
the discontinuity estimator has the smaller error there, and at $1/\tau=1000$ it has
the smaller error at both bandwidths, helped by mean
functions that are linear on each side of the boundary, which removes its bias at
wide bandwidths. The regression-discontinuity estimator is therefore a competitive
and often more efficient way to analyse such logs when the policy is sharp; the
separation concerns which target is learnable and at what exploration cost, and
does not depend on which boundary estimator is used.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The results separate two uses of logs from sharpening policies. If the question is
how outcomes respond where the deployed policy is indifferent, and the operational
cost vanishes at the boundary, a growing deployment permits increasingly precise
local inference, uniformly over a Lipschitz model, while cumulative exploration loss
vanishes; the number of exploratory actions still grows, but their operational cost
falls. If the question is the population average effect, uniformly consistent
estimation requires divergent worst-case exploration loss, and deployment size does
not remove that requirement. Reporting an effect estimated from such logs therefore
requires saying which of the two quantities it is.

For design, the relevant choice is not whether to use temperature but how. With one
temperature shared by all contexts, the probability of the non-preferred action
decays exponentially in the policy's confidence, so reaching confident contexts
forces a temperature that is costly near the boundary; uniform mixing,
context-dependent temperatures or temperature mixtures with a vanishing weight on a
moderate temperature avoid this. Allocating by the gap saves further loss when gaps
are heterogeneous; in the closed-form costs of Section~\ref{sec:sims} the saving
relative to uniform mixing is about eleven percent.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The policy is known and assignments are logged; the score
and preferred action do not learn from outcomes; the setting is a single decision per
unit with independent contexts; the boundary results are one-dimensional; the lower
bounds assume Gaussian outcomes with known variance and exclude models in which
boundary data identify the population effect; the separation requires an operational
cost that vanishes at the boundary; and the treatment is strategy assignment under a
fixed generator, not the realized text. Extending the results to learned scores,
multidimensional scores (for example the conditional effect given $\Delta(H)=0$) and
multi-turn conversations is left open.
